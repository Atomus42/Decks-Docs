## Guiding Principles for Every Slide

3. **3-6 bullets maximum** per slide. Boardroom-clean. No clutter.
4. **Zero speed claims.** No "in seconds," no "18 months to seconds," no absolute percentages without methodology.

## Slide 4: The Yellow Card Gap and the Literature Imperative

**Title:** Why Published Literature Is More Critical Than Ever

**Objective:** Connect the under-reporting problem to the specific value of systematic literature evidence structuring. If spontaneous reporting captures only a fraction of real adverse events, published literature becomes the essential complementary evidence source -- and structuring it systematically becomes a safety imperative.

**Bullets:**
- **94% median under-reporting rate** for adverse drug reactions across 37 studies (IQR 82-98%) (Hazell & Shakir, Drug Safety 2006; confirmed in multiple subsequent analyses)
- Only **10% of serious reactions** and **2-4% of non-serious reactions** reported via Yellow Card (MHRA estimates)
- IMMDS Review 2020 ("First Do No Harm"): the Yellow Card system is **"too complex and too diffuse to allow early signal detection"**
- January 2025 Parliamentary debate: "the yellow card system for reporting suspected adverse drug reactions is failing, with no process for following up on serious or fatal reactions" (Hansard, 16 January 2025)
- **Implication:** With 90%+ of serious ADRs unreported through spontaneous systems, published observational studies, meta-analyses, and case series become the primary evidence reservoir -- but only if they can be systematically structured and cross-referenced

**Suggested figure/diagram:** Two-column layout. Left column: "What Yellow Card captures" -- a narrow bar representing ~10% of serious ADRs, labelled with "1.3M+ cumulative reports since 1963." Right column: "What published literature contains" -- a wider bar representing the vast volume of observational studies, meta-analyses, and case series published across PubMed (37M+ records). Between them, an arrow labelled "Evidence structuring bridges this gap." Source citations below each bar. No alarmism; factual presentation.

**Speaker notes:** This slide connects the under-reporting problem to the evidence-structuring opportunity. Say: "This is not a critique of the Yellow Card system. It is a statement of the mathematical reality that every pharmacovigilance authority faces: spontaneous reporting captures a fraction of real adverse events. The IMMDS review said it directly. Your Parliamentary debate reinforced it. The consequence is that published literature -- observational studies, meta-analyses, systematic reviews -- becomes the essential complementary evidence source. But with hundreds of publications per drug class, structuring that literature systematically is itself a capacity challenge. That is the specific problem ArcaScience addresses." If Allison raises the antidepressant/PSSD example, connect directly: "For a signal like antidepressant-associated persistent sexual dysfunction, where clinical trials were too short to detect it and Yellow Card reporting is sparse, the published observational literature is where the evidence lives. ## Production Notes for Deck Designer

### Visual language
- Colour palette: muted blues, greys, and one accent colour (teal or dark green). No bright reds, oranges, or marketing gradients.
- Typography: clean, sans-serif. No italic emphasis except for direct quotes.
- Every diagram must place the **human assessor** as the central or largest element.
- All "system" elements are shown in muted/supporting colours; all "assessor" elements are shown in prominent/foreground colours.
- No stock photos, no abstract AI imagery, no neural network visualisations. These signal marketing, not substance.
- **Published metrics** should be displayed in a consistent format throughout: bold number, source journal, year. Example: **92% precision** (AI in Medicine, 2025).

### Slide density
- Maximum 6 bullets per slide. No exceptions.
- No bullet should exceed two lines of text at presentation font size.
- If a bullet requires more explanation, move the detail to speaker notes or an appendix slide.

### Language rules
- Subject of capability sentences should be the human assessor, not the AI.
  - Correct: "Assessors receive a structured evidence package enabling them to..."
  - Incorrect: "Our AI identifies 9x more signals than..."
- No absolute claims: no "100%," no "always," no "every" without qualification.
- No speed claims: no "in seconds," no "in minutes," no time comparisons without published methodology.
- No superlatives: no "world's largest," no "most comprehensive," no "best-in-class."
- Use "evidence-structuring platform" or "evidence-structuring tool" as the standard descriptor. Not "AI-driven benefit-risk analysis platform."
- **Every quantitative claim** must include either a journal citation, a case study source, or an MHRA strategy document reference. No unsourced numbers.

### Source citation format
- All data points on slides must have a small-text citation visible on the slide itself (not only in speaker notes).
- Format: (Source, Year) -- e.g., (MHRA People Strategy 2023-2026), (AI in Medicine, 2025), (Hansard, January 2025).
- Full reference list in Appendix D.

### Narrative arc
- Slides 1-4: MHRA's challenge (capacity gap, Yellow Card under-reporting, technology gap)
- Slides 5-6: The evidence structuring gap (where SafetyConnect stops, what ArcaScience does and does not do)
- Slides 7-10: What ArcaScience delivers (workflow fit, pipeline, GPT-4 comparison, traceability)

### Handling Q&A
- Prepare a separate Q&A reference document (not in the deck) with detailed answers to all 50 questions from the Question Matrix.
- If a question is asked that cannot be answered, say so: "That is a question we cannot answer today. We will provide a written response within [timeframe]."
- Never deflect a question by pivoting to a capability claim. Answer the question, acknowledge the gap, then offer the next step.
- **If asked about any published metric:** have the paper available on a laptop to show the relevant section. Do not ask MHRA to take our word for it.
